# Facilitator guide

A session takes 60 to 90 minutes: 10 minutes of briefing, 40 to 45 minutes of injects, 20 minutes of hot wash.

## Before the session

| When | What |
|---|---|
| One week before | Pick the scenario. Send the players the playbooks it uses (listed on the briefing screen), not the injects. |
| One week before | Confirm who plays each role. Use the people who would really decide: the CISO, the CIO, and in-house counsel or the external breach coach. |
| One day before | Open the simulator on the room's screen and check that the page loads. It needs no internet once loaded, so a local copy of `index.html` works too. |
| On the day | Name a scribe. The simulator keeps the decision log, but the scribe captures what was said and who disagreed. |

## Setting the clock

* **60 seconds** for a team that has played before: it reproduces the pressure of a real bridge call.
* **90 seconds** for a standard run.
* **2 to 3 minutes** for a first run, or when the team must agree across functions.
* **Untimed** when the goal is discussion. Timeliness is then not scored.

Use **Pause** when the room needs to clarify a fact, not when it needs more time to decide.

## During the injects

1. Read the inject aloud. The decision owner reads the three options aloud.
2. The other two roles advise; the owner decides. Keyboard shortcuts **A**, **B** and **C** pick an option.
3. The owner ticks who must be informed now. Ask "who would be angry to hear this from the press?"
4. Record the decision. Read the outcome and "What the playbook says" before the next inject.
5. When the team stalls, ask "what does the playbook say?" rather than giving the answer.

## The hot wash

Open the report. Start with the hot-wash questions, then the improvement actions. For each action, name an owner
and a date before people leave the room. Download the Markdown report and attach it to the post-incident review
template from the playbooks pack: an exercise is reviewed the same way as a real incident.

## Reading the report

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Low decision score for one role | That role needs the playbook, or a deputy who knows it. |
| Low timeliness with good decisions | The team knows what to do but decides by committee. Clarify who owns which call. |
| Low communication | Notification lists are not known. Print the RACI and the regulator, insurer and bank contacts. |
| High uninsured cost | Insurer notice, panel firms and backup protection were not known before the incident. |

## Running it again

Rotate scenarios each year, swap the roles so deputies play, and compare scores between sessions. A team that
reaches *Ready* at 90 seconds should try 60 seconds, or the other scenario.
